Theory

This page summarizes the mathematics implemented by FEMTISE.jl: what problem is solved, how it is discretized, and which properties of the result are guaranteed. A detailed account, with proofs and references, is given in the accompanying paper.

The problem

The package solves the Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem

\[-\nabla\cdot\big(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\psi\big) + q(\mathbf{x})\,\psi = \lambda\, r(\mathbf{x})\,\psi \qquad \text{in } \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^d ,\]

which contains the time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE) $\hat H\psi=\varepsilon\psi$ for

\[\hat H = -\nabla\cdot\big(\mathbf{p}\,\nabla\big) + V,\qquad \mathbf{p} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{-1},\quad q = V,\quad r = 1,\quad \lambda=\varepsilon .\]

Physical situationKinetic coefficient $\mathbf p$How to pass it
One particle of mass $m$$\hbar^2/2m$ ($=1/2$ for an electron in a.u.)p(x) = 0.5
Effective mass $m^*(x)$ in a heterostructure (BenDaniel–Duke)$\hbar^2/2m^*(x)$p(x) = 0.5 / mstar(x[1])
Two 1D particles of masses $M_1, M_2$ on a 2D mesh$\mathrm{diag}(\hbar^2/2M_1,\ \hbar^2/2M_2)$p₁, p₂ (see Low-level interface)

The coefficients may be discontinuous. $r$ is kept general because it appears after changes of variables.

Weak formulation

Physical states have finite norm and finite kinetic energy, i.e. they belong to the Sobolev space $H^1(\Omega)$. Multiplying by a test function $v$ and integrating by parts gives the weak problem: find $(\lambda, u)$ with $u \neq 0$ such that

\[a(u,v) = \lambda\, b(u,v)\quad \forall v,\qquad a(u,v) = \int_\Omega \big[(\mathbf p\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v^* + q\,u\,v^*\big]\,d\Omega,\qquad b(u,v) = \int_\Omega r\,u\,v^*\,d\Omega .\]

For regular $u$, $a(u,v)=\langle v|\hat H|u\rangle$ and $b(u,v)=\langle v|u\rangle$. Only first derivatives appear. The forms are built by bilineal_forms.

Boundary conditions. The package imposes homogeneous Dirichlet conditions $\psi|_{\partial\Omega}=0$ (impenetrable walls): they are built into the spaces ($U=V=H^1_0$) and the boundary term of the integration by parts vanishes.

Interfaces. If $\mathbf p$ or $V$ jump across a surface, the weak form automatically enforces continuity of $\psi$ and of the flux $\hat n\cdot\mathbf p\nabla\psi$, i.e. the BenDaniel–Duke conditions $[\psi]=0$, $[\partial_n\psi/m^*]=0$. Nothing has to be imposed by hand.

Discretization

The domain is divided into a mesh of cells (segments in 1D, quadrilaterals or triangles in 2D) of maximum size $h$. In each cell the wave function is a polynomial of degree $\ell$ determined by its values at the nodes (Lagrange elements). The global basis functions $\phi_j$ are continuous, piecewise polynomial and nonzero only on the cells around node $j$. Writing $u_h=\sum_j\Phi_j\phi_j$ turns the weak problem into the generalized eigenvalue problem

\[\mathbb{A}\,\boldsymbol\Phi = \varepsilon\,\mathbb{B}\,\boldsymbol\Phi,\qquad \mathbb{A}_{ij} = a(\phi_j,\phi_i),\quad \mathbb{B}_{ij} = b(\phi_j,\phi_i).\]

$\mathbb A$ (Hamiltonian = stiffness + potential matrix) and $\mathbb B$ (overlap or mass matrix) are sparse and Hermitian, and $\mathbb B$ is positive definite. The components of an eigenvector are the values of the eigenfunction at the nodes, $\Phi_j=u_h(\mathbf x_j)$. The integrals are computed cell by cell with Gauss quadrature exact for polynomials up to a chosen degree $k_q$.

What is guaranteed

Upper bounds. The discrete space is a subspace of $H^1_0$. By the min–max principle, every computed eigenvalue is an upper bound to the exact one,

\[\varepsilon_n \le \varepsilon_{n,h},\]

and the bounds decrease monotonically when the mesh is refined. This holds when the integrals are exact; see Accuracy and best practices for the effect of an insufficient quadrature degree.

Convergence. For eigenfunctions that are smooth on every cell,

\[0 \le \frac{\varepsilon_{n,h}-\varepsilon_n}{\varepsilon_n} \le C_n\,h^{2\ell}, \qquad \|u_n-u_{n,h}\|_{H^1} = \mathcal O(h^{\ell}),\qquad \|u_n-u_{n,h}\|_{L^2} = \mathcal O(h^{\ell+1}).\]

Eigenvalues converge twice as fast as eigenfunctions. The constant $C_n$ grows with $n$ because excited states oscillate faster. In terms of the number of unknowns $N_h \propto h^{-d}$, the eigenvalue error decays as $N_h^{-2\ell/d}$.

Discrete spectrum. Because the domain is a finite box, the spectrum is discrete. The continuum of an open system appears as a pseudocontinuum of box states whose density increases with the size of $\Omega$.

The eigensolver

Only a few eigenpairs of matrices with $10^3$–$10^5$ rows are needed, so the package uses the implicitly restarted Arnoldi method of ARPACK in shift-and-invert mode:

\[(\mathbb A-\sigma\mathbb B)^{-1}\mathbb B\,\boldsymbol\Phi = \nu\,\boldsymbol\Phi, \qquad \nu = \frac{1}{\varepsilon-\sigma}.\]

The eigenvalues $\varepsilon$ closest to the shift $\sigma$ become the largest $\nu$ and converge first. $\mathbb A-\sigma\mathbb B$ is factorized once by a sparse direct method (SuiteSparse), and each iteration costs two triangular solves.

Choosing $\sigma$: a value below the ground state returns the $n_{\rm ev}$ lowest levels; a value inside the spectrum returns the levels around that energy (e.g. a band, or states near a threshold).

ARPACK computes few eigenpairs of large sparse matrices; LAPACK (dense) is preferable only for small matrices or when the full spectrum is needed. Both rely on BLAS.
